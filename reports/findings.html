Enforcement over time

Stops rose while crime fell, and the growth came from stops officers chose to make

Between 2021 and 2025, reported serious crime in the city fell and police 911 calls held flat. Total stops still rose by more than a third. The increase was driven by officer-initiated stops (traffic, equipment and investigative); stops that a 911 call sent officers to actually declined.

A quarter of stop records leave the reason blank. Recovering those by matching them to 911 records makes the rise in officer-initiated stops larger, not smaller: +105.8%, reaching 59% of all stops by 2025.

Race and neighborhood stops

At the same crime rate, neighborhoods with more Black residents were stopped more

In 2025, excluding the downtown core, the city's neighborhoods split into four equal groups by the share of residents who are Black. The two groups with the highest shares recorded exactly the same crime per resident, 56.8 per 1,000. The group that is 42% Black nonetheless saw 84 stops per 1,000 residents, against 60 in the group that is 18% Black: 40% more.

A regression across all 87 neighborhoods asks the same question while holding recorded crime, population, renter share and land area fixed, and accounting for the fact that neighboring areas influence one another. The gap has grown every year. It is statistically clear in 2025 under every check; in 2024 it is present but cannot be separated from income.

Stops and crime

Through 2024, stops scaled with crime

Across neighborhoods, a place with twice the recorded crime had about twice the stops. The crime elasticity of stops sat between 0.97 and 1.06 in every year from 2021 to 2024, never distinguishable from 1. It dips to 0.72 in 2025, but the uncertainty around that estimate still reaches 1, so that is not a confirmed break.

Correction to an earlier draft

An earlier version of this analysis reported that stops grew more slowly than crime (elasticity 0.34–0.78). That model used 911 call volume as a control. Calls are largely a result of crime and move with it almost exactly (correlation 0.92), so they absorbed most of crime's effect. With population in their place, the result above holds and the earlier claim is withdrawn. The dotted line below shows how far the flawed model was off.

Search outcomes

Black drivers were searched twice as often, and those searches found less

If officers need the same level of suspicion before searching anyone, searches of every group should turn something up at similar rates. From 2024 to 2026, Black people who were stopped were searched 2.2 times as often as white people (16.7% vs 7.6%), yet those searches ended in arrest less often: 44.2 vs 51.7 per 100 searches. A lower yield from more searching suggests a lower bar for searching.

The gap appears only where officers had discretion. Across 2018–2026, in stops officers initiated, Black drivers' searches led to arrest 9.4 points less often; in stops a 911 call sent them to, the gap reverses to +3.6.

The direction holds in all 24 arrest-based versions of the test. Counting citations instead of arrests does not reproduce it, and a traffic citation says little about whether a search found anything. An arrest may sometimes come before the search rather than result from it; the records cannot tell these apart.

Gunfire and 911

Most gunfire that sensors detect never draws a 911 call, and willingness to call varies fourfold

ShotSpotter sensors give an independent count of gunfire in the areas they cover. Linking each detection to any 911 call within 400 metres and ten minutes shows that only 11.4% of detected gunfire was also called in. That share ranges from 6.0% in Lind-Bohanon to 23.9% in Powderhorn Park. Because the sensors apply the same yardstick across these neighborhoods, the gap points mainly to differences in readiness to call, rather than in how much shooting there is.

Coverage matters. The sensors cover 20 of 87 neighborhoods (22% of the city's area) but hold 97.6% of all detections. Downtown West has the city's fifth-highest count of confirmed shootings and two sensor detections since 2007, so any citywide comparison built on sensor counts would wrongly read downtown as quiet. Only covered neighborhoods are used here. Matching a random time-shifted copy of the calls gives a 0.22% match rate, so the linked pairs are real, not coincidence.

How this was done

Method and limits

All records were joined to Minneapolis's 87 neighborhood boundaries. Gunfire records were grouped into single events, since one burst of shots often produces several records. Census block-group estimates were apportioned to neighborhoods by area (interpolated city population 426,269). Neighborhood regressions use spatial models, because neighboring areas are not independent. Data problems found along the way were corrected before any result was reported:

  • "Shots fired" is not all sensor data. 58% of those records are 911 calls; only 35% come from sensors.
  • Officers' perceived-race field is unusable. It is 68–74% "Unknown" before 2023, so race as recorded after the stop is used.
  • Use-of-force records changed how they count twice (2023 and 2024), so force cannot be compared across those years.
  • Downtown per-resident rates are misleading. Most people there don't live there; Downtown West runs 218 stops per 1,000 residents.
  • Demographics are held fixed. Census figures are 2019–2023 estimates applied to every year.
  • These are associations. None of the findings establishes why any individual stop or search happened.
